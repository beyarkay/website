## Advice from chatgippity

1. “That sounds like sci-fi / too far away”

   People will lump it in with time travel, flying cars, or “The Jetsons.”

   Likely phrasing: “That’s not going to happen anytime soon,” or “we’ve been
   hearing this forever.”

Counter-strategy: Emphasize trajectory, not timeline. Show how current
capabilities (e.g. GPT-4, Claude, Gemini) already hint at discontinuities.

2. “Humans will stay in control / someone will pull the plug”

   Naive control belief. Assumes a human will always be in the loop.

   Also appears as “We’ll just give it rules” or “Just don’t hook it up to the
   internet.”

Counter-strategy: Use aligned analogies (e.g. “Even you don’t understand all
your thoughts—you think we’ll debug a mind millions of times faster?”)

3. “Why would an AI want to harm us?”

   Anthropomorphizing. Assumes intent requires malice.

   Often voiced as: “It’ll be benevolent,” or “It won’t want anything.”

Counter-strategy: Instrumental convergence. Simple goals like “maximize
paperclips” converge on eliminating obstacles (i.e., humans).

4. “Aren’t humans the real problem?”

   Political deflection. People channel climate change/inequality concerns into
   this.

   Appears as: “We already have nukes/climate change/racism, why worry about
   AI?”

Counter-strategy: Stack risk frames. “We already have threats we understand—do
we want to add a faster, less-understood one that can amplify the others?”

5. “Experts aren’t panicking, so why should I?”

   Deference fallacy. Trusts authority not reporting the problem loudly.

Counter-strategy: Mention which experts are worried (e.g., Stuart Russell,
Geoffrey Hinton, OpenAI alignment teams). Avoid crank territory.

6. “Even if it’s true, I can’t do anything”

   Learned helplessness.

   Often ends with: “So why worry?”

Counter-strategy: Reframe as moral clarity, not control. “You don’t need to solve it, just be one of the people who noticed.”
