### Selfish behaviour, selfless outcomes

It is possible for selfish behaviour to lead to selfless/altruistic actions.

### Some examples

- Sheep: simple, clear, reducing risk, one-step
- Pelotons: human-related, reducing risk, two-step(?)
- Truckers
- IONITY
- 1800s ultra-wealthy spending money on public works. There's a false
  dichotomy where we think either the billionaires must spend their money
  privately, or give it all away to the public never to be seen again, but it's
  possible to align the incentives such that billionaires _want_ to spend their

### Three options for obtaining more sparse resources

If you are in a competitive environment with other agents, and you have to
spend some energy in order to acquire some resources, you'll typically have one
option:

1. Spend your energy trying to get more of the resources. Typically, these
   resources are scarce and so you'll have to spend energy fighting off your
   competition as they also try to acquire these resources.

but in addition to this option, there's often two more options:

2. Spend energy trying to increase the absolute quantity of scarce resources,
   keeping relative proportions the same. This will typically incentivise
   cooperation, since increasing the absolute quantity of the resource will
   make everyone wealthier and so energy spent competing is less effective than
   energy spent increasing the total quantity of resources.

3. Spend energy trying to change other agent's desire for the fixed resources,
   thereby reducing the competition for the fixed pool. This usually looks like
   some form of negotiation: you attempt to show that the resource isn't
   actually what the other agent wants, so that you can acquire more of the
   resource with less competition.

These three factors (increasing how much stuff you've got, increasing how much
stuff everybody's got, and convincing everyone to not desire your stuff) are
the ways in which a competitive system can resolve itself.

### Two sides of the same coin: increasing risk, decreasing reward

Competitive cooperation can happen either by parties collaborating to increase
the total reward, or by parties collaborating to reduce the total risk. These
are equivalent, although finding one phrasing to represent both of them is
tricky.

### Why cooperative competition is beneficial

These systems that incentivise competitive cooperation have some common traits
that make them worth examining in detail:

1. **self-enforcing**: there doesn't need to be any external reason to do the
   thing, all agents are incentivised to maintain the system

2. **distributed**: there doesn't need to be any single agent enforcing good
   behaviour, all agents are motivated to behave cooperatively and to reward
   the cooperative behaviour of others.

3. **efficient**: These systems incentivise agents to spend their energy
   increasing the total amount of wealth available, instead of spending energy
   stealing wealth from other agents. This allows agents (which would otherwise
   be competing) to pool resources towards increasing the total amount of
   wealth more effectively.

4. **self-realising**: Cooperating in these systems is a _dominant_ strategy,
   in that each agent will get a better reward by cooperating, regardless of
   what the other agents choose. If the agents figure out that they can make
   these moves, they will do so.

The above points make these systems worth trying to realise in the real world,
they are an improvement on existing competitive systems.

## Real world applications: Converting existing competitive environments

I theorise that we can take existing competitive environments and make them
more cooperative. For example, the discovery of pelotons before they were
introduced to cycling. How would you have discovered this, what insights would
have been required to see that this is a thing.

## One-step vs two-step benefits

Sometimes the beneficial action is a single step, such that taking the action
that helps yourself is the same as the action that helps the group. These are
self-sustaining and don't need cooperation nor trust between parties.

Sometimes it requires multiple steps so that you might inconvenience yourself
today for the promise of your partner helping you out tomorrow. These require
cooperation and trust, and so are prone to coordination failures.

## Cooperative competition

Two things can be true at the same time:

1. You can be competing against an enemy, attempting to beat them at some game
   or competition
2. Your best action, the one which benefits you more than anything else you
   could do, might end up also helping your enemy.

The cyclist joining a peloton does so because they benefit from the reduced
drag, but doing so inadvertently assists their competitors. Cyclists are happy
to help out their competitors because both they and their competitors gain
about the same amount. Likewise, the lone sheep wants to join the herd because
it reduces the risk, and this lone sheep is "competing" with the other sheep in
the herd in that a wolf will probably only take one sheep. The herd is happy
for the lone sheep to join, because having extra sheep in the herd reduces the
risk for all sheep in the herd.

It's worth keeping in mind an example in which everyone loses out because
there's no selfishly-motivated altruism, such as large military forces: a
military is only useful because other people have militaries that might attack
you. Everyone wants to have the biggest military, so everyone attempts to
increase the size of their army.

Looking at these examples, they all have some downside that they're trying to
minimise: the sheep want to minimise the risk of being eaten, the cyclists want
to minimise the energy lost to drag, the militaries want to minimise the damage
caused by someone else invading. They also have some competition dynamic
between the member of the group: a wolf will probably only eat one sheep, so
each sheep would rather someone else get taken. Only one cyclist can come
first, so no cyclist wants to make the others' lives easier. More powerful
militaries beat less powerful militaries, so everyone :

Given these dynamics, there's three main ways the sheep or the cyclists can
spend their energy in order to minimise the downside while not losing the
competition:

### 1. Compete

Compete with the other players for the existing resource. This assumes that the
"pie" of existing resources is fixed, and that the only thing you can affect is
your _share_ of the pie. For you to gain, someone else must lose, leading to
competition between the players of the game.

### 2. Cooperate

Attempt to "increase the pie", trying to find either better ways to extract the
resource resource or new sources of the resource. This can lead to cooperation,
because by increasing the size of the pie, everyone gains proportional to their
original share of the pie, and so everyone is incentivised to try and increase
the size of the pie.

### 3. Negotiate

Attempt to reduce the other's desire for the pie. This is negotiation:
attempting to gain more of the pie by showing your competitors that they don't
actually want the pie.

## Trucker platoons & win-win scenarios

Let's explore these methods of getting more resources with a new example:
truckers driving the same route along a highway. If there's two trucking
companies which transport goods between two major cities, then these
companies will compete with one another for the same customers. In addition to

time and money into transporting their goods better, because their competitor
is trying to steal their market share. Even the best transport company will
fail if nobody knows it exists. The companies are forced to divert some time
and money towards defending their slice of the pie.

There's a phenomenon through which you can compete against another person while
realising that your best move will (incidentally) help them. This move is the
best because it helps _you_ more than it helps _them_, but it helps them
nonetheless. In some settings, the best way for you to help yourself will end
up helping your enemies.

In the above examples, working together increases the total rewards (or
decreases the total risk), while working individually will necessarily steal
resources from your competition:

- With IONITY, the demand for electric vehicles will increase if there's better
  infrastructure.
- With cyclists, racing individually cause each of them to bear more total drag
  than if they all cycled in a peloton.
- With the herd of sheep, it's not that the amount of good things increase, but
  rather the amount of bad things decrease: Sheep in a herd have less total
  risk than sheep all spread out individually.

Imagine a zero-sum game like poker: You can only win if someone else loses. The
rules of the game do not permit you to gain anything without taking it from
someone else. Given that the rules of the game are fixed, there's no way to
incentivise cooperation between players even if you want to. _However_, if it's
possible to change the rules of the game, and to do so in a way that allows
everyone to gain by working cooperatively, then we don't _need_ to incentivise
cooperation because cooperation becomes the best action for any player to take,
regardless of if they're thinking only about themselves or about the good of
the group.

You might be shaking your head saying that you can't just _change the rules of
the game_. But you're wrong. Nobody complains about pelotons being against the
spirit of cycling. By many measures, they're the most interesting part of the
race! The rules of cycling say that everyone's competing which would imply that
cooperative pelotons are against the rules of cycling, but there's a weird
local maximum in which cyclists are incentivised to cooperate. Who's to say
there aren't other similar maxima, in other games, just waiting to be
discovered?

So this leads us to say that there are three ways in which games can go: play
the game (leading to wasteful competition), change the rules (leading to
selfish cooperation), or change the players (e.g. negotiation):

#### Item 1: Compete over who gets more of the good stuff or less of the bad stuff A

Globally, you and your opponent are just throwing energy into a big pit. If you
spend X amount of energy building a trap, your opponent has to spend ~X energy
defeating the trap (assuming both you and your opponent are approximately
equally skilled). Note that your energy and your opponent's energy basically
cancel each other out, so there's no limit to how much you and your opponent
will waste trying to best one another. This dynamic leads to run-away
competitions like Mutually Assured Destruction, military industrial complex,
wasteful capitalistic spending.

This is typically what's solved for in Game Theory. Game theory assumes the
game is constant and solves for the agents' best actions

- If there is no way to change the pie (and no way to negotiate and therefore
  change the players), then we have a zero-sum game. Note that true zero sum
  games rarely exist, the real world has no rules and even the rules you think
  exist can still be bent. Pelotons exist, even though they're in a zero-sum
  game. But often real-world zero-sum games have permissible adaptions that
  create benefit without any cost. (e.g. cyclists or birds in formation)
  There's no guarantee that the benefit (TODO: what's the benefit? This is to
  abstract) will last, or that it'll be shared by all players of the game, but
  it _is_ possible to take a real-world scenario that appears zero-sum and to
  convert it into something where, on average, everyone benefits. "permissible
  adaptions" meaning here that nobody's going to complain (or at least, they
  won't complain _much_) if you make a change to the game in a given way (TODO
  this is important! Emphasise). Like the cyclists forming a peloton: there's
  nobody claiming that it's _unfair_ to form a peloton, even though it changes
  the game so much that not forming a peloton would be suicidal in modern
  cycling. Cycling is a zero-sum game that still has positive-sum strategies.
  Even though the cyclists are competing for limited resources (places on the
  podium), they're still able to be more efficient _as a group_ and this is
  positive sum and something they'd all prefer to do, because "defecting" (e.g.
  not joining a peloton) is not a rational strategy.

- These systems aren't perfect, there will be times when they collapse back
  into competition (e.g. cyclists approaching the end of the race will break
  apart pelotons) but they are an improvement on the default
  everyone-for-themselves strategy that often emerges. The individuals aren't
  _perfectly_ aligned with everyone else, there are situations in which they'll
  take actions against everyone else. But it's useful to note that this
  behaviour is possible _at all_. This points towards the idea that we might
  design a system that intentionally creates these behaviours.

- So selfish behaviour is enough to incentivise selfless actions, although the
  actions are not _maximally_ selfless: A maximally selfless sheep would
  actively search for the predator and offer themselves up. But I think we can
  agree that getting somewhat selfless behaviour out of selfish animals is
  pretty neat.

#### Item 2: Work to increase the total good stuff or decrease total bad stuff B

This is magical because if you spend X units of energy, then you benefit by
some amount proportional to X. And so does your opponent. So your opponent can
also _independently_ choose to spend X units of energy, and then you also
benefit. Instead of spending 2X and getting nothing, you spend 2X and get
~2X!

This requires a change of perspectives, you have to take agency and have to
possess the theory-of-mind to suggest a better policy for your counter-party.

This is typically what's solved for in Mechanism Design. Mechanism design
assumes the agents' policies are constant and solves for the game.

- Something that's also good is how distributed this approach is. You don't
  need any higher-tier reward system that monitors everything and encourages
  selfless behaviour (e.g. governments, financial incentives, bonuses for
  productive employees). You just need everyone to act in their own
  self-interest (which they'll already do) and selfless behaviour is what you
  get. You don't need to exert energy trying to get everyone to play nicely
  together, because if they're any good at what they do, they'll end up playing
  nicely regardless.

- The fact that this works without any understanding of the reasoning behind it
  makes me very hopeful that it could work in highly distributed systems where
  you don't have much hope of telling everyone how to play the game (such as
  global economies, AI alignment, etc).

#### Item 3: Reduce the other's demand for the good stuff/increase demand for bad stuff C

This works out to changing the other person's policy to something that's
beneficial to yourself while keeping the rules of the game the same. Only
advanced multi-agent reinforcement learning typically studies the effect one
agent's policy will have on another's, so this is also often ignored.

This is basically good negotiation skills in business. Being able to rephrase

  same supplier as their competitors. This is also like TSMC being the chip
  producer for competing companies. The ISS is kinda like this, it's very
  expensive to host a space station but globally competing countries still
  cooperate in this local field.

  - If costs are externalised to a 3rd party, then cooperation between
    competitors will only be so much as is required by that 3rd party.
    There _is_ cooperation between competitors when required by a 3rd party
    (e.g. chip designers sending things to ASML), but it doesn't feel like
    "true" competition. Although it's unlikely for the cooperative competitors
    to defect. Also, not all things can be sent to a 3rd party, it's impossible
    for cyclists to get someone else to handle the problem of aerodynamics, but
    it is possible for tech companies to get someone else to handle the problem
    of internet brosers

  - many supplier companies are like this actually. They're supplying
    competitors, but deliberately stay out of their consumers competition.
    So even though you're competing with someone, you might have the same
    supplier and both support that supplier, even though supporting that
    supplier helps your competitor.

- This is a bit like merchants congregating in a market together? Individually
  the merchants would only be able to access the people who come to their shop,
  but if they setup a market with a bunch of their competitors then they get
  more foot traffic than the sum of foot traffic each merchant might get
  individually. e.g. food courts, restaurants congregating on one street, car
  salesmen gathering on one street, gold/diamonds/jewellery collecting
  together, art galleries, financial services.

- Q: Why not just aim for globally-selfless _and_ locally selfless?
  A: Well, it's a lot easier to design a self-sustaining system where the
  actors in that system are self-motivated, rather than trying to globally
  coordinate.
  This is a bit like asking "why can't everyone just get along". Sure, everyone
  would be better off if everyone got along and cooperated. But this is not
  sustainable. There's not been a single time in which people have "just got
  along" for any meaningful period of time. Above and beyond that, what if you
  could design a system that would work, regardless of whether or not people
  got along. Also, the system is self-enforcing, we don't require any higher
  power to ensure everyone gets along, because everyone is self-motivated to
  get along.

#### Appendix (or Delete)

- There's an unstable point where two entities need
  to interact, and they can either mutually increase the friction between them
  or they can mutually decrease the friction between them. e.g. government
  agencies might mutually increase the amount of paperwork required to do
  things because generating and processing paperwork is a _competitive_ cost
  (e.g. if I pay X, you must pay something proportional to X or risk losing
  out) and not a _collaborative_ cost (e.g. I gain by paying X, and you gain
  proportional to my gain). E.g. this is like government agencies increasing
  the paperwork needing to be sent between themselves and not getting any
  actual work done.

- This behaviour by the corporations can partially be explained by Joel
  Spoelsky's writing about how companies are incentivised to [commoditise their
  complement][1]: Google can both increase its profits if the internet is cheap
  and fast, because if the internet is fast, more people will use Google's
  products. Because of this, Google is incentivised to make the internet as
  cheap and as fast as possible. Google is happy cooperating with their
  competitors, so long as the internet gets faster and cheaper because that'll
  be good for them in the long run.
  However, the idea of commodotising your complement doesn't fully explain the
  cooperative behaviour of competing corporations. Also, these examples lead us
  to think this behaviour is only present between corporations. But that's not
  the case.

- You might complain that animals are just doing what they're instincts tells
  then to do. That they only have low-level drives like "be near other sheep",
  without any notion of the reasoning behind that drive, or why being in a herd
  is a good idea. This seems largely true, depending on which herd animal
  you're talking about. It also doesn't affect the argument, if anything it's
  _more_ impressive that you can force animalistic drives to act selflessly
  without any exterior force encouraging that behaviour.

- You might argue that there exist psychopaths in the world who would just take
  themselves down if it meant that you'd go down with them. They're outliers,
  and tend to sort themselves out.
