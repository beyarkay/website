- Proctoring is shit
  - For timed/scheduled assessments or meetings, give candidates "dummy"
    assessments so they can get familiar with the layout, the proctoring
    software.
  - Encourage candidates to record their screens as a guard against proctoring
    mishaps
  - For some of them (proctorio), I had to use cell phone data for 3 hours of video call and screen recording because it wouldn't let me connect using my wifi, and was absolutely useless in helping troubleshoot.
  - Proctorio also required me to take a video recording of the room I was in
    _after the timer had started_, wtf? there's no point in doing this, it just
    makes things difficult.
- Don't have computer-enforced hard word limits that prevent a candidate from
  submitting a 1200-word essay with 60s left to remove 5 words from
  _somewhere_.
- Allow candidates multiple choices in answering. Let them take timed assessments multiple
  times. If you have two questions you consider equivalent, allow them to
  answer any one of them.
- Make it clear what the commitment is and what will be provided. Will the next
  round of the applications require more feedback/questions/forms to be filled
  in?
- Give two weekends for submissions
  - Don't give me bullshit about not having enough time, or having an
    especially punishing
- Basically all fellowship applications (CAIF, MATS, MARS, Astra) had identical
  deadlines. Why! That just means the most promising candidates (who got into
  multiple fellowships) have to split their time.
- Most applications are very good about not being overly America-centric, using
  the anywhere-on-earth timezone. This is appreciated.
- Most applications were very bad about being northern-hemisphere centric. The
  effects aren't as bad, but it's weird seeing DEI disclaimers about equality
  but then having the applications be in "winter".

- Okay I get that you want your applications to be at the end of the Northern
  hemisphere school year, but that seems bizarre that you're going to make the
  most talented of the bunch (who got into multiple fellowships) all end up
  performing worse because they've got to ration their time brutally, and don't
  have all the time they need to fill out their applications?

- MARS, for unknown reasons, had an initial application that was around 1000
  words long (precisely, 4900 characters). Candidates who passed this round
  were given about seven days to look through the profiles and project
  descriptions of 18 mentors and complete the applications that they wanted to
  enter. It took me around three hours just to read them all and figure out who
  I was a good match with, and then each mentor required around three hours of
  preparation work in order to complete their application (many had an explicit
  time limit of two hours, but that was after you had read any material they
  wanted your feedback on). This is no judgement on the mentors! They should be
  free to place as much selection as they feel fit. But giving seven days to
  complete will inevitably reduce the observed competence of the applicants,
  and this seems pointless. Applications should be hard! but they should be
  hard because they have high academic standards, not because of arbitrary time
  pressure.

  I don't see why applicants couldn't have been made aware of these second
  round questions earlier. MATS does this very well, in my opinion. They have
  an initial all-of-MATS application, and then each mentor stream has their own
  specific applications, all of which are handed in at the same time.

  (for my local time, I received the invitation for the second round at
  20:50 on October 20th, and the final deadline was 14:00 on October 27th).

- Dates and times: many fellowships said that "Applications close on XXth
  September", without giving a time. This is nearly useless! Please give a
  time, and please make that time in the Anywhere on Earth time zone.

- Denial letters (especially towards the later end of the applications) are
  really hypocritical. They'll be phrased positively, like "our team identified
  you as having strong potential for AI safety research" and "We are genuinely
  disappointed that we couldn't find a match for you this time, as we were
  impressed by your profile", but refuse to give any individual feedback. I
  feel an organisation that has successfully identified competitive candidates
  would do more to help their careers than wish them on their merry way.

- Many of the denial letters (MARS, also another?) did give links to other
  resources, which is nice. But to be honest some sort of letter of
  recommendation saying "we have evaluated this candidate and regretted being
  unable to give them a position" (_if_ this is in fact true) would be good and
  AFAICT low risk.
