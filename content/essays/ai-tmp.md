A lot has been written about the potential impact of AI (or more specifically,
white-collar job automation), but the implicit assumption is that the audience
is American. The vast majority of the world. The US made up around 25% of the
world's GDP in 2023[^1], which is impressive but far from a majority.

NOTE: this essay only looks at job automation due to existing AI. It does not
theorise about future developments, nor any progress that might be caused by
usage of AI in various fields. It does not, for example, look at the health
impact due to AI doctors being better able to keep people happy and healthy. It
only thinks about a world where we have the current AI, just better deployed
into the world.

## Some jargon

For the purposes of this essay, I'll be considering AI which has the ability to
automate any work that could be done remotely or largely remotely. I'll
consider jobs which undergo large changes in their day-to-day operations to
have been effectively "automated" and replaced with a new job, even if the
title or position for that job remains the same. There are many industries
which might become more efficient under AI automation, but would show
resistance because society values having flesh-and-blood humans in those roles,
either because we like interacting with people (high-touch sales
representatives, end-of-life care), or because we value having a human to blame
at the end of the day (CEOs, politicians, the judiciary system), or because the
particular job is uniquely positioned to pass regulation that prohibits their
job being automated (lawyers, politicians, regulators).

For example, a sales representative who still wines-and-dines clients in person
but does so with an AI advisor in their ear advising their every quip and
comment, I'll still consider that original sales job to have been automated by
AI since the job has changed so significantly. This is a fuzzy boundary, you
can draw a near-infinite number of examples between the points "tech-free sales
person" and "salesperson as a human meat-puppet" and I won't be able to draw a
line in the sand saying which job positions are fully automated and which are
still the original role, but this isn't crucial to the core argument.

## UBI is not universal

To get this out of the way, Universal basic income is often cited as a solution
to mass unemployment due to job automation by AI. The core idea being that
efficiency gains due to AI will make the corporations using or providing these
AIs more profitable. This profit will feed back into the economy, if not via
salaries (because the corporation has few human employees) then via taxes to
the government.

This might work well for the countries in which these corporations are paying
their taxes, but what about the rest of the world? If a South African company
uses an American AI to automate some work, this South African company will pay
money to the American company, and this money will be taxed by the American
Internal Revenue Service (IRS), but not by the South African Revenue Service
(SARS). SARS _will_ tax the South African company on their increased profits,
but will not be able to tax the foreign AI labour.

So we can expect the amount of revenue available to the South African
government to be driving close to zero as more work is automated. We also
assume that AI companies will be more profitable than their non-AI
counterparts. So the South African government will get less money if the
increased tax revenue due to AI companies is less than the decreased tax
revenue due to a lack of personal income tax.

The actual numbers from the [2024 national budget review][1] (see Table 4.2
Budget review) show that revenue due to personal income tax is 37% of the
total, while corporation income tax is 17% of the total. The other major
category is "Domestic taxes on goods and services" which seems to include VAT,
fuel, levies, customs, sugar & sin taxes, and this comes out to 35% of the
total. The remaining 11% is various small items.

So income tax is a major proportion of South Africa's tax revenue, and existing
corporations would need to become 3x as profitable in order to remove the need
for personal income tax. This number is a thumb-suck, in reality not every job
can or will be automated by AI, not every company can or will replace their
workers with AI. And this only looks at the impact on the government,
completely excluding the impact on the individual.

mass automation will need to be shared with the citizens of the nation.

## Job automation is based on the cost of labour

The cost of labour varies from country to country, but it is especially high in
the US, the UK, and the EU. High cost of labour is a large incentive to
replace that labour with some alternative. We have already seen something like
this happen with many US companies "off-shoring" some tasks to workers in low
cost-of-living countries.

If a white-collar labour replacement comes onto the market, it will find more
adoption in countries with a high cost of labour since those jobs are more
profitable to automate. It is likely that countries with a low cost-of-labour
will watch countries with a high cost-of-labour become automated, while they
themselves remain largely powered by human labour. This is unlikely to remain
the case for long, prices for AI products have historically been driven down
incredibly quickly, and it's likely that AI workers which are fiscally
uncompetitive one year will become a no-brainer decision the next year. But
countries with a low cost-of-labour and their governments will get to watch the
AI replacements happen in other countries first, and may be in a better
position to enact good policies based on the mistakes of the other countries.
