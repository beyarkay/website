Basically every idea that doesn't have widespread approval comes under this
banner of a risk-reward trade-off, but they're rarely discussed on this
meta-level. For example, Going To College is the option that raises your worst
possible outcome (since having a degree makes it more likely you'll get _some_
job) but the detractors of a college education will say that it limits you
because you won't be able to have full freedom for those four years, and so
won't be able to achieve your maximum. But most people still choose to go to
college over founding a startup straight after high school, because most people
hoping their careers optimise their average performance, not their maximum
performance.

This is often phrased as a risk-reward trade-off, where you can get larger
reward (at the price of higher risk) or you can accept a lower reward, and not
incur as substantial a risk. But I think this perspective misses an aspect of
decision making where sometimes you're maximum performance is significantly
more important than your average performance, but sometimes you're average
performance is more important. And viewing decisions with this lens can help
make the decision easier to figure out.

For example, when looking for an apartment, you don't care about the average
quality of the apartments you view, because you're only going to be living in
one of them. You're trying to optimise the maximum. So the strategy here is to
view as many apartments as possible, since you only need _one_ apartment to be
a good match in order to be happy living there.

On the other hand, most companies are judged by their average performance. It
doesn't matter if they had one good quarter if the rest of the year was
terrible. Most companies need to never be terrible, rather than sometimes be
brilliant.

It's much more common for people to be optimising for the average quality of a
thing, these are the everyday tasks like doing chores or talking with friends.
It's so much more common, that often we don't realise when we should be
ignoring the majority of our attempts in order to succeed maximally at one
of them.

In dating, you only care about getting along with one person, so the average
quality of your dates doesn't matter. It only matters that you click perfectly
with one person.

Coming up with an idea for a start-up is also a field in which you only care
about your maximum performance. It doesn't really matter if you have 1000
shitty ideas, only that you had one idea that's brilliant.

This effect should change how you go about doing your search, because if only
your maximum performance matters, you should be trying to take as many shots as
you can.

There's something special about putting in extraordinarily more effort than
your competitors, especially when they're significantly better than you are at
the thing your competing over.

Often it's difficult/time consuming to improve the quality of your average
attempt, but increasing the number of attempts you make is significantly easier,
and almost mechanical.

---

There's something special about "just trying more often", because it can
become unreasonably powerful when you are pit against people better than you.
If someone else is just naturally more talented than you, they're going to win
if the game is one where you're judged based on average performance: even if
sometimes you get lucky, their average performance is better than yours, and
so over time statistics will beat you down until you can bear it no longer. The
more talented candidate will always win on average in the long run.

_But_, if the game is one where you're judged based on your maximum
performance, and you're allowed as many attempts as you care to complete, then
you can guarantee a win regardless of how much better your opponent is than
you^[3]. You might need to try many, many, _many_ more times than your
opponent, but you _can_ beat them.. If your opponent only makes one attempt
(which is surprisingly likely, in my experience), then even if that attempt is
very good, you can try 20 or 40 times, and the more you try, the greater your
odds are of getting lucky. But it doesn't matter that you "just" got lucky,
because you are judged on your maximum performance.

One thing, is that at all costs you need to increase the variance of your
attempts. It's no good making 1000 attempts if they're all basically the same.
You _need_ variance and diversity in order to get lucky.

### The randomness of the game isn't interesting

When I use "games" as the thing people are competing in, it's easy to think
that the player is constant but they're exploiting the randomness in the game
in order to get lucky and win. This is a true phenomenon, but it's not what I
want to discuss.

I think it's more interesting when there's randomness in how the player chooses
to perform that can lead to a win. A player who uses the same strategy 100
times is only exploiting the randomness in the game (and this randomness is not
necessarily biased in their favour), but a player who tries different
strategies 100 times is better off because they're exploiting the randomness in
the game as well as in their own playing of the game. And their differing
strategies are more likely to be biased in their favour than the randomness
inherent in the game is likely to be biased in their favour because the player
is able to discard random strategies that obviously are disadvantageous.

---

### It's not interesting when you can improve the quality of the output with practice

There's also something about creative pursuits, or games where you can get
better over time. The painter who makes 1000 paintings is likely to
improve over time, because each painting will likely get slightly better as
they refine their technique. Even an absolute beginner who creates 1000
paintings might start to approach long-admired master painters, if the master
painter isn't attempting to improve their own work. This phenomenon is also
true, but it's not what I'm trying to get at. I'm more interested in games
where the cause of variance isn't due to something you can control, but due to
external factors.

## Games of interest

There are many places this idea applies, but specifically it applies to
situations where:

- Your attempts at completing some specific task fall on some distribution of quality
- You can, by choice, increase the variance of this distribution
- Some completed tasks are higher quality than others
- You can attempt the task multiple times
- different people when attempting this task have their attempts fall onto
  different distributions. Some people are better than others at the task.

This idea doesn't really apply to people who are beginners, where there's a lot
of skill they can gain by practising. This is more applicable to people who
have reached diminishing returns on practice, but still need to compete

Additionally, the following need not be true:

- You don't need to be competing against someone else
- There doesn't need to be random variance

The requirement for variance is critical here. If you're getting the same
(unsatisfactory) results, then you're probably not being creative enough with
what you do. You need more diversity. Yes, this will end up with _worse_ ideas,
but it will also end up with slightly more good ideas. And if you're judged by
your maximum, only the good ideas matter.

Consider the problem of populating your résumé with the things you've done:
you've done a lot of things, and some will appeal to some potential employers,
others will appeal to others. Should you try to cover all your bases, including
a little bit for everyone, or should you narrow in and focus on one particular
field that you know you enjoy?

The generic form of this problem comes up quite frequently: You can either
get mild approval from a large number of judges, or you can get strong approval
from a much narrower range of judges.

For example, when on a first date, it's a common failure mode to try be
inoffensive, to say nothing that anybody could disagree with, to suppress the
more of-kilter aspects of your personality in fear of scaring away the person
in front of you. And why shouldn't you? The whole _point_ of going on a date is
to go on another date, right? And scaring away the person because your guilty
pleasure is eating Marmite with jam isn't going to get you another date.

Unfortunately, this approach misses the forest for the trees. The point of
dating is to eliminate the vast majority of people until you're left with one
person you couldn't bare to be without. You're don't actually care about the
opinions of basically everyone you date, you need one person to be on your
wavelength. And tempering your opinions will only make it more difficult to
find someone who shares the opinions you tried to hide.

But it's not always the case that you should alienate the majority in the hopes
of pleasing one person. Sometimes you want the opposite: most public-facing
businesses cannot afford to attend to some niche group, it's simply not
profitable enough[^2]. But it's not only when sifting through large numbers of
people that this dynamic comes up: most jobs will evaluate you on your
sustained performance over a long period of time, and not on one stellar
performance two years ago. In this environment, you're incentivised to have
consistent progress that's not too bad, and you're not really incentivised to
have one incredible day that's then followed by weeks of unproductive
wandering about.

Ceilings and floors: you can either have many kids kinda liking maths (but very
few mathematical savants) or you can have many kids despising maths but some
kids absolutely adoring maths. And I'd argue that specialisation is the thing
we need here. Breakthroughs don't come from 1000 people who take mathematics in
their first year. Breakthroughs come from the 5 people who completed their
Maths PhDs.

And this speaks to a greater problem: Breakthroughs come from a small number of
specialists, not from a large number of generalists[^1]. And if we focus
society on producing specialists, then a society which encourages breakthroughs
must necessarily have many specialists in their own fields complaining that "if
only there were more of us". These specialists are directionally correct in
their laments, but they don't see that the cost. To encourage breakthroughs, we
want to maximise the performance of the 99th percentile (the "ceiling"), we
don't want to maximise the performance of the 50th percentile. If you've been
through the AWS SDE grind, this percentile-based thinking will be common
knowledge to you.

It isn't always the case that we want to maximise the 99th at the expense of
the 50th. Global health is an example where we really don't give a fuck if the
people at the 99th percentile of health are doing really well despite the vast
majority of the population suffering in pain and dying of curable maladies.
This is a case where the 1st percentile (the "floor") really matters.

But
it's often phrased as do X or do Y, despite the reality being that we want to
raise the floor. It's generally a good idea to ensure that nobody can be
suffering in pain and malnourished, even if that means that little Timmy has to
endure his headache for an extra couple of hours. Of course this isn't the
explicit choice that you or I make, but Moloch is hard at work here making
these decisions for us. People in the bottom percentile of health (or wealth
for that matter) generally don't have the bargaining power to improve their
situation (and definitely not to the same degree as people in the top
percentile have the power to improve their situation). So if you're the unlucky
1 in 100 with the worst health, you're at the mercy of those above.

Another example of this would be high level vs low level programming languages.
High level languages generally result in a higher median performance; it is
hard to write really terrible code in a high level language due to the
safeguards implied by the language being high level. Low level languages
(assembly, C) generally have a higher maximum performance, but lower minimum
performance. You can really cause some problems if your Assembly is poorly
written or incorrect, but you'll struggle to default while writing Fizz Buzz
in Python. The natural caveat for programming languages, is that they suffer a
promotion towards incompetence.

It's interesting to think how AGI will affect this dynamic. Maybe the median
researcher at a frontier lab has good ideas 2% of the time. If they can work in
parallel, then good ideas will quickly come out of the woodwork.

I guess more generally, having many many attempts at a problem is (IG) more
parallelisable than just being really good at something. In part, because I
think there are more people who have a lower average intelligence but who can
occasionally "burst" to a good idea than there are really smart people who
consistently have good ideas.

CTOs say they only hire the best and that hiring good people is
really really hard, but then also say they'll replace their workers by AI "very
soon". Intuition says you can't replace one brilliant employee by 2 pretty good
employees, but I wonder where the break even point is. 10 pretty good
employees? 50? I'm not afraid to say one brilliant employee _cannot_ be
replaced by any number of sufficiently mediocre employees. It seems like Jeff
Dean just will have better ideas in an idea-space so dramatically unlike
anybody else.

Another point, is that AIs are fairly homogeneous. Trained on approximately the
same training data and RLHF'd in approximately the same way, there is some
variation to their thoughts, but I think the very best employee has ideas so
far from those of the competition, and that's what gives the best their edge.
They can see solutions that the competition just wouldn't even consider.

Now it's important to note that 1. I don't think AI will remain this
homogeneous, only that they currently are quite homogeneous. Future models will
have more varied thoughts and come up with more varied solutions than current
models. And 2. not every employee is Jeff Dean. By definition, not every
employee is the cream of the crop, and so as the human employees become less
and less impressive and AI candidates become more and more Jeff Dean, I can see
a time where maybe no number of AIs could replace your star coder, but maybe 50
employees could replace Jeff who rounds the corners of buttons when it's
fashionable to do so and makes them square again when the rounded buttons
become naff.
